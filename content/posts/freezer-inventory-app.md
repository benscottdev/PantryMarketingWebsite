A freezer inventory app has one job worth doing: record the date each thing went in. Not a countdown to a “defrost by” date, because there is no sourced date to count down to. The [Food Safety Information Council](https://www.foodsafety.asn.au/freezer-storage-times/) is direct that frozen food stays safe indefinitely from a bacterial standpoint and that quality, not safety, is the practical limit. So the number Pantry attaches to a frozen item is the only defensible one: how long it has been in there.

## Why there is no defrost-by countdown

Every fridge item on Pantry’s list carries a window, [taken from CSIRO’s published guidance at the cautious end](/resources/where-pantrys-expiry-dates-come-from). The freezer gets no such window, on purpose. The “chicken freezes for N months, mince for M” chart that circulates online has no source anyone can point to, and [our freezer guide](/resources/freezer-times-explained) declines to reproduce it for that reason. What the Food Safety Information Council does publish is about the appliance, not the food: a standard fridge-freezer holds quality for a few weeks, a chest freezer at -18°C for three months or more depending on fat content. A countdown built on a chart nobody can check would be decoration, and Pantry does not do decoration with a font.

## What Pantry tracks instead

Pantry’s list runs across three places, fridge, pantry and freezer, and an item keeps its identity as it moves between them. The chicken thighs scanned off Saturday’s receipt start in the fridge with three days on them. Mark them as frozen on Sunday and the fridge countdown stops applying. What shows instead is the date they went in, so the question in three weeks is not “how old is this” but a number on the list: frozen since the fourteenth. The item stays on the household’s list the whole time, which is the part that stops someone buying another pack of thighs on Wednesday.

## The freezer is where food goes to be forgotten

A fridge announces its failures. Spinach wilts, milk turns, and the smell forces a decision. A freezer does the opposite. Nothing in it ever looks urgent, and a bag with no label and a coat of frost can sit behind the peas for a year without anyone being able to say what it is or when it arrived. That is not a safety problem, per the Food Safety Information Council. It is a quality problem and a memory problem, and the memory problem is the one an app can fix: a dated line for every bag, so the mystery bag is the mince from the fourteenth and gets used before the bag frozen this week.

## Freeze on day one, and let the list remember

Every shelf-life post on this site gives the same advice: if the week is uncertain, freeze it the day it comes home, before the fridge window closes. The freezer list is what makes that advice cheap to follow. Freezing early stops costing a memory, because the list remembers, and [getting it back out](/resources/defrosting-chicken-safely) starts from a prompt rather than an archaeological dig. Move it down tonight, cook it tomorrow.

## One freezer, one list, every phone

A chest freezer in the garage is the most shared and least seen storage in a house. Nobody opens it to browse. With Pantry’s household, the frozen list is [the same on every phone](/resources/six-phones-one-pantry), so the flatmate at the shop on Thursday can see there are two packs of thighs in the garage before buying a third, and the person cooking tonight can see the bolognese frozen a fortnight ago without lifting the lid. When Pantry ships, the freezer gets the same treatment as the fridge: not a guess about what is in there, and not an invented date, but the one fact worth recording, written down on the day it happened.
