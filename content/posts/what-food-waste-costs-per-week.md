$2,500 a year is the Fight Food Waste CRC’s figure for what the average Australian household spends on food that ends up binned, via [End Food Waste Australia’s fact library](https://endfoodwaste.com.au/fact-library/). A yearly number is easy to file away as an abstraction. Divide it by 52 and it stops behaving: about $48 a week, every week, straight off the grocery budget. That is a streaming bundle, a tank of petrol, a family takeaway — recurring, and cancellable.

The reason it never feels like $48 is that it is never charged as $48. It leaves in pieces, across an ordinary week, none of them big enough to register as a decision.

## A week of it, itemised

No two households waste identically, but the shape is familiar enough to narrate. Monday, half a bag of spinach goes soft behind the milk — [it only ever had five to seven days](/resources/wilting-spinach-five-dinners). Wednesday, the leftovers from Sunday quietly pass [their three-day window](/resources/how-long-do-leftovers-keep) while takeaway gets ordered instead. Friday, the second carton of milk — bought because nobody was sure there was a first — starts its slide toward [the end of its five to seven days](/resources/how-long-does-milk-last) unopened. Saturday’s clean-out finds a cucumber liquefying in the crisper and a tub of yoghurt nobody remembers buying.

Each item is a shrug. Two dollars here, six there, nine for the mince that never became bolognese. It takes the Fight Food Waste CRC’s other finding to explain how the shrugs add up unnoticed: households bin more than twice what they believe they do. The waste is real; the perception runs at less than half scale.

## Why the weekly frame changes behaviour

A $2,500 annual figure invites a once-a-year resolution. A $48 weekly figure invites a different question: what would you do to stop a $48 direct debit you never agreed to? Nobody answers that with “nothing.”

The weekly frame also matches how the waste happens. Groceries are bought weekly, [produce turns inside a week](/resources/how-long-food-lasts), leftovers live three days. Food waste is a weekly-cycle problem, so the money is best counted on the same cycle — and fixed there, one week at a time, not through an annual overhaul.

At the measured exchange rate — $2,500 over 265kg is roughly $9.43 a kilogram, both figures per the CRC and FIAL via End Food Waste Australia — saving five kilograms of food across a week is about $47 recovered. One rescued shop’s worth of near-misses is the whole weekly leak, plugged.

## Cancelling the subscription

The fix is not eating differently — [the grocery-savings post covers this at length](/resources/save-money-on-groceries-without-buying-less) — it is seeing the week’s deadlines before they pass. Three habits carry most of the weight:

- **Know the week’s expiry order.** Dinner planning that starts with “what turns first” beats dinner planning that starts with “what sounds good” by roughly the cost of whatever turned.
- **Claim the leftovers a day early.** The three-day window is generous right up until it is not; day two is the reliable day.
- **Count your own week, not the average.** [The waste calculator](/calculator) turns your household size and spend into your own weekly figure, working shown.

Pantry is built to run that first habit automatically: a photographed receipt becomes a dated list, the list sorts itself by what turns next, and the morning digest tells the whole household what this week’s $48 is about to be made of — while it is still food, and still money.
