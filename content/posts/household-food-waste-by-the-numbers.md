Ask someone how much food their household bins in a year and most people guess low — not narrowly low, either. The research says households throw out more than twice what they think they do. That gap between the guess and the number is what this post is about.

## The number is $2,500

The Fight Food Waste CRC puts household food waste at about $2,500 a year. FIAL's *National Food Waste Strategy Feasibility Study* puts the weight behind that at 265kg a year per household — 2.46 million tonnes nationally, close to a third of everything Australia wastes. Both figures come from [End Food Waste Australia's fact library](https://endfoodwaste.com.au/fact-library/).

$2,500 is not what a careless household spends. It is the average — which means plenty of households that consider themselves careful are most of the way there without knowing it.

## Household figures, not supply-chain figures

$2,500 and 265kg are household figures — what leaves your kitchen. A separate, much larger figure — $36.6 billion and 7.6 million tonnes a year — covers the entire food supply chain: farms, transport, retail and households combined. The two get quoted together often enough that households end up wearing a number that is mostly not theirs. Farming, transport and retail account for most of it. Households are a real slice, not the whole pie — and $2,500 a year is still real money out of a real grocery budget.

## Nobody decides to waste $2,500

No household sits down and allocates part of the grocery budget to the bin on purpose. The $2,500 is built from small, ordinary moments: a bag of spinach behind the milk, the second half of a lasagne nobody remembered, mushrooms bought for a recipe that changed. None of it looks like waste at the time. It only adds up to $2,500 in hindsight, at the end of a year nobody was keeping count.

## Where it goes

There is no single villain category. It spreads across:

- **Fresh produce** — the shortest shelf life in the shop, and the first thing to go soft in a crisper drawer nobody checks daily.
- **Leftovers** — cooked once, eaten never, because "tomorrow" loses to takeaway most weeks.
- **Dairy** — milk that turns a day before anyone notices, cheese pushed to the back of the shelf.
- **Bread** — bought fresh, forgotten, then binned or frozen too late to help.

None of these are unusual mistakes. They are the ordinary shape of a busy week, repeated fifty-two times a year. Our [breakdown of where the $2,500 goes](/resources/where-the-2500-goes) covers each category in more detail.

## Why the guess is always low

The Fight Food Waste CRC is specific about the size of the gap: people under-report their own waste by more than half. Part of it is memory — you remember buying the spinach, not throwing it out three weeks later, because nobody marks the moment food goes in the bin. Part of it is that waste happens in small increments across the week rather than one dramatic bag, so it never feels like $48 a week while it is happening.
