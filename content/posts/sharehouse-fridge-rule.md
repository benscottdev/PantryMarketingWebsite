A sharehouse fridge fails in a way a family fridge never does: nobody’s food is technically anyone else’s problem. That should mean less waste — everyone only answers for their own groceries — but it means more, because nobody has full visibility into what is in there, or how old it is, except the person who bought it.

## The mechanics of the problem

In a family fridge, one person usually does most of the shopping and roughly knows what is in there. In a sharehouse, three or four people shop separately, on different days, for different meals, and the fridge turns into a shelf of individually owned items nobody has the full picture of. The bag of spinach at the back could be a week old or three weeks old, and the only way to know is to ask whoever bought it — which nobody does, because asking feels like an accusation.

So it sits. The fridge slowly fills with small, individually reasonable purchases that add up to a food-safety experiment nobody signed up to run.

## The rule: label it, or lose the argument

The most effective sharehouse fridge rule is also the simplest: if you do not want to defend throwing something out, write the date on it when it goes in. Not elaborate labelling — a piece of tape and a date. It solves two problems at once. Anyone in the house can tell at a glance whether something is fair game to bin, and nobody has to ask “is this yours, and is it still good” every time they open the door.

The rule sticks in houses that apply it to everything, not just their own shelf. A fridge where only one flatmate labels things is a fridge where only one flatmate’s food gets a fair hearing.

## Why this matters more in a sharehouse than a family home

A family fridge has one shared budget behind it — waste is everyone’s money, indirectly. A sharehouse fridge has four separate grocery budgets sitting in the same physical space, so waste is specifically someone’s chicken breast or someone’s sourdough loaf, gone. The stakes are higher, not lower — even though the emotional temperature around fridge conversations in a share house runs the other way, with everyone avoiding the topic because raising it feels awkward.

A visible, shared system removes the need to raise it at all. Nobody has to say “your milk’s gone off” out loud if the app flagged it three days ago, to everyone, at once.

You do not need buy-in from every flatmate on day one. Start by labelling your own shelf. A shared digital list still works with only two of four housemates on it — it just covers less ground until the rest catch on.
