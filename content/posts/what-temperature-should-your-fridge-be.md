Every storage window on this site — [milk’s five to seven days](/resources/how-long-does-milk-last), [chicken’s three](/resources/chicken-in-the-fridge-three-days), [eggs’ three to six weeks](/resources/how-long-do-eggs-last) — rests on one assumption that almost nobody checks: the fridge is cold enough. [FSANZ](https://www.foodstandards.gov.au/business/food-safety/keeping-food-at-the-right-temperature) puts the food safety danger zone at 5°C to 60°C, which makes the fridge target simple. 5°C or below, everywhere food sits.

A fridge running at 8°C is not slightly warm. It is holding everything in it inside the danger zone, full time, and quietly shortening every window in [the shelf-life reference](/resources/how-long-food-lasts) without changing anything you can see.

## The ten-dollar answer

The dial inside most fridges is not a thermometer — it is an unlabelled scale from “less cold” to “more cold” that says nothing about the number food safety cares about. A basic fridge thermometer from the supermarket settles the question in a day. Put it in a glass of water on the middle shelf, read it after a few hours, and you know something the dial never told you.

If it reads above 5°C, turn the fridge down a step and check again the next day. That is the entire procedure.

## Warm spots and cold spots

One reading is a start, not the whole picture, because a fridge is not one temperature.

- **The door is the warmest zone** — it swings every time it opens, which is why this site keeps milk and [eggs](/resources/how-long-do-eggs-last) off it.
- **The back of the lower shelves runs coldest** — the right home for meat and anything with a short window.
- **The crisper drawers are their own climate**, designed to hold humidity for produce rather than to be the coldest place.

A packed fridge changes the map again. Air has to move for cold to reach the middle of the load, and a fridge stacked solid after a big shop can hold warm pockets for hours. That is worth knowing on the day of the shop itself — the day most fridges work hardest.

## When the door habit matters

Every door opening trades cold air for room air. One cook who opens the door eight times making dinner is normal life, and a fridge recovers. A door left ajar against an overfull shelf overnight is the version that costs something — the fridge spends the night in the danger zone and nothing looks any different in the morning. If milk starts turning days earlier than [the five-to-seven CSIRO gives it](/resources/how-long-does-milk-last), the fridge is the first suspect, not the milk.

## Why this is the cheapest fix on this site

Most food-waste advice costs effort every week. This one is a thermometer, one adjustment, and it pays out on every single item the fridge ever holds — because a fridge at 5°C delivers the full window every shelf-life table promises, and a warm one silently taxes all of them at once.

Pantry’s countdowns are built on the same assumption CSIRO’s figures are: a fridge doing its job. Get the temperature right and the dates the app attaches to a scanned receipt mean exactly what they say. It is the one part of the system only you can calibrate.
