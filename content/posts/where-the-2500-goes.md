$2,500 a year is the Fight Food Waste CRC’s figure for what an average Australian household bins, and 265kg is FIAL’s figure for the weight behind it — both collected at [End Food Waste Australia’s fact library](https://endfoodwaste.com.au/fact-library/). No household keeps an itemised receipt for what it throws out, so the figure is a research estimate, not a till roll. But the shape of where it goes is not a mystery. It follows the foods with the shortest shelf life and the highest turnover in an average trolley.

## Fresh produce takes the biggest hit

Fruit and vegetables have, by a wide margin, the shortest windows of anything in a standard shop. Leafy greens turn in five to seven days, and mushrooms in as few as two, per Sustainability Victoria’s guidance behind [our shelf-life reference](/resources/how-long-food-lasts); tomatoes and herbs are not far behind. All of it is bought weekly by almost every household, so the chance for a punnet or a bag to be forgotten resets every single week, all year.

Produce is not wasted because people care about it less than anything else in the trolley. It is wasted because it has the least margin for a bad week — a late night at the office, dinner out that was not on the plan, and the crisper drawer takes the loss.

## Leftovers are the second-biggest quiet loss

Cooked food has a [three-day window](/resources/how-long-do-leftovers-keep) per the NSW Food Authority, and “we’ll have it tomorrow” is one of the most common sentences said over a container in an Australian kitchen — usually followed by takeaway instead. The leftovers were not a bad decision going into the fridge. They became waste through inertia, three days later, when nobody claimed them for a meal.

## Dairy and bread round out the list

Milk that turns a day before anyone notices. Cheese pushed behind everything else on the shelf. A loaf that goes stale before the second half gets used. None of these is a large loss on its own, but they happen every week, which is how small amounts compound into a real annual figure.

## What is notably not the main driver

Pantry staples — rice, pasta, tinned goods, flour — last for months, sometimes over a year, and rarely show up in the $2,500 at all. The waste sits almost entirely in the short-shelf-life categories: produce, dairy, meat and cooked food. A household that wants to fix this gets the most out of watching those categories, not the pantry cupboard.

## Why the number holds even for careful households

The Fight Food Waste CRC’s research found that households under-report their own waste by more than half — not because people are being dishonest about it, but because waste happens in amounts too small to notice one at a time. A wilted bag of spinach here, a forgotten yoghurt there. Nobody adds those up in real time, which is exactly why the yearly total surprises most people the first time they see it written down.
