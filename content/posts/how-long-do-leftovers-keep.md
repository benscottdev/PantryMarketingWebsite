“We’ll have it tomorrow” is one of the most common sentences said over a container of leftovers, and it is usually true. The question is how many tomorrows that covers before the plan needs to change to “cook something else” instead — and that is exactly the question Pantry answers the moment the container goes in the fridge.

## The number: three days, two for rice

The [NSW Food Authority](https://www.foodauthority.nsw.gov.au/consumer/special-care-foods/leftovers) puts most cooked leftovers at three days once refrigerated. Cooked rice and pasta get a shorter window — two days — and that is not a rounding difference. Cooked rice in particular can carry bacterial spores that survive cooking and multiply during cooling and storage, so the shorter number reflects a real difference in risk, not extra caution for its own sake.

Both numbers assume the food went into the fridge reasonably promptly after cooking, and stayed there.

## The two-hour and four-hour rule that starts the clock

Before the fridge-storage clock even starts, there is a separate rule about how long cooked food can sit out. The NSW Food Authority’s two-hour/four-hour rule says food held between 5°C and 60°C for up to two hours can go back in the fridge safely; between two and four hours it should be eaten straight away rather than stored; past four hours, cumulative, it should be thrown out. That time is cumulative across every occasion the food sits out — dinner on the table, then again when it is served as lunch the next day — not reset each time.

A dinner left out on the table for three hours before anyone thinks to refrigerate it has already used most of its safe window before the three-day fridge clock even begins.

## Why three days, not “until it smells off”

Unlike raw milk or wilting spinach, cooked leftovers do not always announce spoilage clearly through smell — the cooking process changes how bacteria present, and a container that still smells fine can be past the safe window. This is why the number matters more than the nose here: trust the three days, not a sniff test.

## Reheating matters as much as timing

The NSW Food Authority’s guidance is to reheat leftovers until steaming hot — at least 60°C throughout, not just warm. That is what kills bacteria that may have grown during storage; a container that is merely warmed through has not had the same safety margin as one reheated properly. It is a small extra step that decides whether the three-day window is a safe guideline or a hopeful one.

## What this means for a batch-cooked week

If you cook a large batch on Sunday intending to eat from it across the week, three days covers Sunday through Wednesday — not the whole week. The fix for a five-day meal-prep plan is the freezer for the back half: portion out what will not be eaten within three days and freeze it immediately, rather than stretching the fridge portion past its window. ## Where Pantry closes the gap

Leftovers are one of the biggest quiet contributors to household food waste — cooked once, meant to be eaten, then not, simply because the three-day window closed before anyone circled back to it. Alongside [wilting produce](/resources/wilting-spinach-five-dinners), it is one of the two categories behind most of the [$2,500 a year](/resources/household-food-waste-by-the-numbers) an average household bins.

Pantry treats a container of leftovers exactly the way it treats a fresh grocery item: logged with a date and a countdown the moment it goes in, so “we’ll have it tomorrow” comes with an actual deadline attached instead of an open-ended intention.
