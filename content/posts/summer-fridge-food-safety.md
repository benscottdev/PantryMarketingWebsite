Pantry’s shelf-life countdowns — like every number on this site — assume food goes from shop to fridge in reasonable time. CSIRO’s guidance behind them does too, and so do Sustainability Victoria’s and the NSW Food Authority’s. Summer is the season that quietly breaks that assumption: a car boot in full sun, a slower walk home, a longer stretch on the kitchen bench before anyone gets to the fridge. None of that counts as “reasonable time” on a genuinely hot day, and the maths behind every countdown on this site shifts accordingly.

## The danger zone does not move. Your time inside it does.

[FSANZ](https://www.foodstandards.gov.au/business/food-safety/keeping-food-at-the-right-temperature) sets the food safety danger zone at 5°C to 60°C — the band where bacteria multiply fastest. That range is the same in January as it is in July. What changes is how much of an ordinary afternoon falls inside it: a 20-minute walk home with the shopping, a car boot in direct sun, a kitchen bench before anyone gets around to the fridge. On a mild day, none of that matters much. At 35°C, all three push groceries hard into the zone the CSIRO windows on this site assume they never entered.

## The two-hour/four-hour rule matters more once it is hot

The [NSW Food Authority’s two-hour/four-hour rule](https://www.foodauthority.nsw.gov.au/help/managing-potentially-hazardous-foods) is blunt: perishable food sitting between 5°C and 60°C is safe to refrigerate or eat within two hours, should be eaten straight away between two and four hours, and goes in the bin after four — cumulative, across every stretch it spent out of the fridge that day. In a 20°C kitchen, forty minutes on the bench barely dents that allowance. In a 38°C car, the same forty minutes spends a much bigger share of it, because the food starts hotter the moment it leaves an air-conditioned shop and heats faster from there.

The rule itself does not change in summer. How fast you burn through it does.

## The classic mistake: the hot car

Leaving groceries in the car for a second errand is the single most common way perishable food gets pushed deep into the danger zone in summer. A car parked in direct sun heats up far faster than the air outside it — meat, dairy and anything else potentially hazardous is sitting in an oven, not a warm room. If the second stop cannot wait, the esky comes out, or the shopping comes inside. Ten minutes in a hot car is not the same ten minutes as ten minutes on a kitchen bench, whatever the trade-off feels like in the moment.

## What needs the extra caution

Not everything carries equal risk. Raw meat, poultry, dairy, cooked leftovers and anything built on egg or cream are exactly what the danger-zone rules are written for — see [Pantry’s shelf-life reference](/resources/how-long-food-lasts) for the specific windows once they are back in a properly cold fridge. Pantry staples, whole fruit and unpeeled vegetables shrug off a hot afternoon far better, and summer caution is wasted on them.

## Fridge performance slips in summer too

A fridge working against a hot kitchen and a hotter house does not hold 5°C as reliably as it does in cooler months, especially if it is older or the door keeps opening for cold drinks. A ten-dollar fridge thermometer earns its keep specifically now — it turns “the fridge is probably fine” into a number you can check. Every shelf-life window on this site, and every CSIRO figure behind it, assumes the fridge is holding 5°C or below. Summer is when that assumption most needs checking.

## The practical version

Shop on the way home rather than as the first stop of a longer errand run when it is hot. Bring an insulated bag for the meat and dairy aisles specifically. Get perishables into the fridge inside the safe window, not “eventually.” None of this is dramatic — it is the same ordinary discipline every season asks for, just with a shorter runway before it matters.

Pantry’s countdowns assume the trip from shop to fridge went the way it is supposed to. A hot afternoon spent in the boot is exactly the kind of thing that shortens a window before a shelf-life countdown ever gets to account for it. Trust what you can see and smell over any shelf-life estimate when the two disagree, and see [how those windows get calculated](/resources/how-long-food-lasts) for what “reasonable time” is doing in the background.
