Every expiry-tracker app has to solve one problem before it can help with anything else: how a carton of milk or a bag of spinach gets onto its list in the first place. The alert, the shared view, the suggestion for what to cook — all of it sits on top of that single entry point, so it is the first thing worth checking before you download anything. Get that step wrong and the app becomes one more thing to remember on top of the shopping itself, which is exactly the habit it was supposed to replace. Two more things decide whether the app earns a permanent spot on your phone: whether the whole household sees the same list, not just whoever did the shopping, and whether a use-by date turns into a plan for tonight or just another notification to dismiss. Pantry is built around a photographed receipt to answer all three at once, so that is where this guide starts.

## Where items come from: receipt, barcode or typed in by hand

Most trackers put you to work one item at a time. [NoWaste](https://www.nowasteapp.com/) combines barcode scanning with photo recognition and runs on both iOS and Android, but every item in the trolley still gets logged individually. Pantry Check goes further the other way: manual entry only, no scanning of any kind, which makes it the plainest interface in the category. A full trolley typed in by hand, one use-by date at a time, is a real ask on a weeknight when the shopping still needs unpacking and dinner still needs cooking — the exact moment logging usually gets skipped, which is also the moment the list stops being accurate.

Pantry starts somewhere else entirely. Photograph a Coles, Woolworths or Aldi receipt and the whole shop becomes a dated list in one pass, reading the abbreviations those dockets already use — “WW FF MILK 2L” becomes full-cream milk, not a code you translate yourself. A full shop is one photo, not a barcode scan or a typed line for every item afterwards, which is usually the exact moment — bags on the bench, hands full — an app that demands more effort per item loses you for good. [How Pantry’s receipt scanning sits next to the barcode apps building toward the same idea](/resources/receipt-scanning-apps-compared) covers this in more depth.

## Whether everyone in the house sees the same list

A tracker that lives on one phone only tracks what that one person remembers to log. [Fridgely](https://fridgelyapp.com/) and [KitchenPal](https://apps.apple.com/au/app/kitchen-pal-food-pantry-app/id1084982489) both build sharing in — Fridgely as shared access for family or flatmates alongside its expiry alerts, though it is iOS-only, and KitchenPal as family sharing across a wider platform that also covers grocery lists and meal planning, priced at $5.99 a month for its Premium tier, per its own listing. Sharing has become close to standard in this category, which makes it worth checking how a shared list gets filled day to day, not just whether the feature exists on a pricing page.

That is where the entry point and the sharing feature meet. A list that only updates when someone remembers to add to it stays one person’s job to maintain for the rest of the house, sharing feature or not. Pantry Pro’s household sharing sits directly on top of the receipt scan instead — up to six people on one subscription, all seeing the same list the moment a receipt is photographed, not after someone gets around to typing it in for everyone else. [The household sharing post](/resources/sharehouse-fridge-rule) covers what that looks like day to day.

## Whether a use-by date turns into dinner

A date on a screen is a fact, not a decision. Knowing the mince turns tomorrow does not cook it — something still has to turn that fact into “spaghetti bolognese, tonight” before the mince goes off unused. KitchenPal reaches furthest toward that step of any app in this category, with recipe discovery and meal planning built in alongside its inventory tracking. Most dedicated trackers, NoWaste and Fridgely included, stop at the alert and leave the “so what do I cook” part to you.

Pantry’s free plan includes two meal generations a week, built from whatever a receipt has already logged, plus a morning digest that surfaces what is turning soonest across the household. Pro raises the generations to unlimited and adds an afternoon nudge that names a specific ingredient on the day it needs to become dinner, rather than a generic reminder to check the fridge. The point is to close the gap between “this expires soon” and “here is what to cook with it”, so the alert is a step in a chain and not the last one — a red icon next to “spinach” at 5:30pm on a Tuesday is not much use if nobody has decided what that spinach is turning into.

## What free covers, and where the paywall sits

Most trackers pair a limited free tier with a paid upgrade, and the free tier is where the real test happens — a household will keep using whichever app clears its bar without paying, long enough to decide if the paid version is worth it. NoWaste’s free tier covers six lists and up to 500 items, per its own listing, which is generous room to run a full household inventory without a subscription. KitchenPal’s free layer lacks much of what sits behind the $5.99 Premium tier described above.

Pantry’s free plan is not a trial with a countdown. It stays free for as long as you want it: one receipt scan a week, two meal generations a week, the morning digest, and expiry tracking, no card required. Pro removes the weekly caps, adds the afternoon nudge, and opens up household sharing, available inside the app rather than sold on this website. The free plan alone is enough to see whether a photographed receipt beats whatever the household is doing now, before deciding if it needs more than that.

## What to check before you download anything

A pricing page will tell you whether a feature exists. It will not tell you how it feels to use on a Tuesday night with a full trolley to unpack. Before committing to any tracker, worth checking:

- How many taps, scans or photos it takes to log a full shop, not just one item
- Whether everyone in the household sees the same list, and what that costs per person versus per household
- What happens after the alert fires — a recipe suggestion, a specific nudge, or just a red icon you have to act on yourself
- Whether the shelf-life numbers behind each date have a source you could check yourself, the way [our own reference table](/resources/how-long-food-lasts) carries CSIRO and Australian food-safety guidance on every row
- Whether the app expects scan-by-scan effort for as long as you use it, or asks for that effort once per shop
- Whether the free tier is generous enough to prove the app is worth keeping before anyone in the house has to pay for it
