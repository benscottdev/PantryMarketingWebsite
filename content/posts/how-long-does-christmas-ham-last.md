A whole leg of ham outlasts almost everything else on the Christmas table, and not by as much as people assume. [CSIRO’s refrigerated-storage guide](https://www.csiro.au/en/research/production/food/Refrigerating-foods) gives whole hams two to three weeks in the fridge. Sliced ham — the deli packet, or what comes off the leg once it is carved — gets four to five days on the same guide. This site holds both at the short end and says so: two weeks for the leg, four days for the slices.

## Two clocks on one ham

CSIRO’s delicatessen line is specific: fermented salamis, bacon and whole hams keep for two to three weeks, compared with sliced luncheon meats, which keep four to five days after purchase. The difference is the cut surface. A whole leg has its skin and its cure between the meat and the air. A slice has neither, and it turns on a clock closer to fresh meat’s than to the leg it came off. CSIRO’s sentence is about sliced luncheon meats. This site reads slices carved at home the same way, because a cut surface is a cut surface, and says so here rather than quietly assuming it.

The practical rule follows from that. Carve what the table will eat and leave the rest on the bone. Every slice taken off the leg moves from the two-week clock to the four-day one, whether or not it gets eaten.

## How long does ham last in the fridge?

Two weeks for a whole ham on the bone, four days for sliced ham, both counted from the day it came home. A leg bought on the twentieth of December is a third-of-January item. A packet of sliced ham bought the same day needs to be finished by Christmas Eve. Where the packet or the leg carries a use-by date that lands earlier, [the use-by date wins](/resources/best-before-vs-use-by).

Keep the leg cold the whole time. CSIRO’s advice for food you want to keep a long time is the coldest part of the fridge, which means the back of a low shelf, not the door and not the garage fridge that runs warm.

## Christmas Day changes the clock

None of the above survives Christmas lunch untouched. Once the ham has been carved, plated and left on the table through presents and a long afternoon, what comes back off the table is cooked meat that has been handled and left out, and it is on the same clock as the turkey beside it: [three days, per the NSW Food Authority](/resources/how-long-do-leftovers-keep), with the two-hour/four-hour rule already eating into it before it reaches the fridge. [The Christmas leftovers post](/resources/christmas-leftovers-what-keeps) covers that rule in detail. The short version is that slices which sat out for four hours are not two-week ham any more. They are leftovers, and they may already be past it.

The leg itself, if it went back to the fridge within a couple of hours and was only ever carved from, stays on its two-week clock. The slices that sat on the platter do not.

## Freeze the rest before the window closes

A Christmas ham is bought for a table, not a household, so there is usually more than two weeks of eating on it. Slice what will not be finished, freeze it flat in dated portions, and it becomes February sandwiches instead of a hard decision on the third of January. [Freezing before the window closes](/resources/freezer-times-explained) is the whole trick.

## Where Pantry closes the gap

A Christmas ham is the one item in the fridge running two clocks at once, in a week when the household is bigger than usual and nobody is sure who carved it last. Pantry is built to hold both. The leg is dated from the receipt with its two-week window. The slices that come off it on Christmas Day are logged as leftovers on their own three-day clock, the same way any container of leftovers is. And every phone in the house sees both, so the guest who opens the fridge on the twenty-ninth is looking at a number, not guessing from the smell.
