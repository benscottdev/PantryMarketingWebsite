Pantry’s whole job is watching the clock on what is in your kitchen. That clock starts before any app sees the item — with where it lands the moment you get home. Put a tomato in the crisper drawer instead of the fruit bowl and it loses flavour faster, no receipt scan involved. This is the easiest win on the entire site: no app, no tracking, just putting things in the right place to begin with.

## What should stay out of the fridge

Per [Sustainability Victoria’s storage guidance](https://www.sustainability.vic.gov.au/recycling-and-reducing-waste-at-home/avoid-waste/food-waste/guide-for-storing-fresh-produce):

- **Bananas** — the fruit bowl, away from other fruit. Bananas release ethylene gas that speeds up ripening in whatever is sitting near them.
- **Tomatoes** — the fruit bowl, not the fridge. Refrigeration measurably dulls tomato flavour, which is the specific reason this one is worth remembering even though tomatoes look like an obvious fridge item.
- **Avocado** — the fruit bowl until ripe, then the fridge shelf once it has reached the point you want to eat it. This is the one exception on the list where the *right* storage location changes partway through the fruit’s life.
- **Potatoes** — a cool, dark place, ideally a cloth bag, kept away from onions (onions accelerate sprouting in nearby potatoes).
- **Onions and garlic (unpeeled)** — a cool, dark, dry place. Refrigeration introduces moisture that shortens their life rather than extending it.
- **Whole pumpkin** — the pantry, at room temperature, while it remains whole. Once cut, it moves to the fridge like any other cut vegetable.
- **Whole pineapple** — the fruit bowl. Like pumpkin, this changes once it is cut.
- **Basil** — a glass of water on the bench, not the crisper drawer. Basil is one of the few herbs that dislikes fridge temperatures outright, and blackens faster in there than at room temperature.

## What should be refrigerated

Nearly everything else: leafy greens, berries, broccoli, carrots, celery, corn, citrus, mushrooms, most fresh herbs other than basil, stone fruit once ripe, sweet potatoes, zucchini, and any of the room-temperature items above once they have been cut or peeled. If it is not on the list above, the fridge is the right default.

## Why this list looks arbitrary but is not

The pattern is not “fruit stays out, vegetables go in” — pumpkin and potatoes are vegetables that stay out, and berries are fruit that go in. The real logic is about what each item is built for: tropical and subtropical produce (bananas, pineapple, avocado before ripening) suffers in cold temperatures, and produce with a protective skin built for long storage (whole pumpkin, onions, potatoes) does not need refrigeration’s main benefit — slowing decay in something with no protective barrier of its own.

## The practical version

The single biggest fix for most kitchens is simple: get bananas, tomatoes, potatoes and onions out of the fridge and into a bowl or a pantry shelf. Those four are the most commonly mis-stored items, and fixing just those four covers most of the benefit without needing to memorise the entire list.

## Where this fits with everything else

This post is about *where*, not *how long* — for the shelf-life windows once produce is stored correctly, see [our full reference](/resources/how-long-food-lasts). Correct storage and correct timing work together: a tomato in the fruit bowl still has about five days before it turns, per Sustainability Victoria, and a tomato in the fridge loses flavour on top of that same clock still running. Getting the location right does not stop the countdown — it just stops making the problem worse than it already is.

Once everything is sitting in the right spot, that is exactly the countdown Pantry is built to watch. Every item a receipt adds carries the same shelf-life data as this page, counting down to the day it needs using, not the day you gave up remembering it was there.
