Most of the food waste on this site traces back to a single structural fault: the fridge is shared, but the knowledge of what is in it is not. [Two people buy milk on the same day](/resources/shared-shopping-that-works). [Four flatmates each own a mystery shelf](/resources/sharehouse-fridge-rule). Whoever scanned the receipt knows the mince turns tomorrow, and nobody else does. Pantry’s household exists to remove that fault, and its design choices are worth walking through, because each one answers a real kitchen argument.

## One list, not six copies

The household is built around a single shared pantry — not six personal lists synced together, but one source of truth every phone reads. When a receipt is scanned, the items land on the household’s list with their dates attached, visible to everyone at once. When the milk gets finished, it is gone from everyone’s view, not just the phone of whoever drank it. The point is that no member ever holds private knowledge about shared food: the [two-bottles-of-milk problem](/resources/how-long-does-milk-last) is, at root, an information asymmetry, and a single list is the shortest path through it.

The same goes for the morning digest. It is built to land on every phone in the household, not just the scanner’s — because [the mince turning tomorrow](/resources/mince-going-off-tonight) is not a secret worth keeping, and the person with time to cook tonight is not always the person who shopped on Sunday.

## One subscription, six people

Pantry Pro covers a household of up to six on a single subscription. One person pays and becomes the admin; everyone else joins by invite, free. The alternative — each member needing their own paid plan to see the same fridge — punishes exactly the households that benefit most, and quietly guarantees that at least one flatmate never joins, which [re-breaks the visibility the list exists to create](/resources/sharehouse-fridge-rule). A shared fridge is one thing; it gets one plan.

## Invites are deliberate

Joining a household is by invitation, and the bar is meant to be the same as the physical one: this list shows everything your kitchen holds and everything it is running out of, so let in the people you would hand a house key to. And because houses change, membership does too — leave a household, or remove someone, whenever the share-house reshuffles. The list is a tool, not a group chat you can never mute.

## Built for the argument at 5:30pm

Every one of these decisions targets the same moment: early evening, fridge open, and the question of what to cook colliding with the question of what is about to go off — answered differently by each person in the house, from memory. A household where six phones show one dated list does not have that argument. It has a sorted answer, the same on every screen, and [the $48 a week that argument was costing](/resources/what-food-waste-costs-per-week) stays on the grocery shelf where it was earned.
