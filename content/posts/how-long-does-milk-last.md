Milk lasts five to seven days in the fridge, per CSIRO’s refrigerated-storage guide, and opening the carton does not reset that clock. The date on the pack is a manufacturer’s estimate for an unopened, correctly refrigerated bottle — not a verdict on the one that has been sitting on your middle shelf since Tuesday. Checking by smell before checking the date is the right habit. Here is what pushes milk to the short end of that window, and the signs worth trusting over the print on the carton.

## The shelf life of milk: five to seven days

CSIRO’s [refrigerated-storage guide](https://www.csiro.au/en/research/production/food/Refrigerating-foods) gives milk five to seven days once it is in the fridge — the same range in [our shelf-life reference](/resources/how-long-food-lasts). CSIRO’s number assumes a fridge at or below 5°C and milk that went in promptly. Everything below is about what eats into it.

## Once opened: the same five to seven days

Opening the carton does not reset the window and does not start a new one. Milk keeps ageing whether the carton is sealed or not — opening it just gives air and bacteria more chances to get in, which is why an opened carton belongs at the short end of the range rather than the long end. Count from the day the milk went into the fridge, not from the day the seal came off.

## Full cream, skim and low fat: the same answer

Full cream, reduced fat, low fat and skim all get the same five to seven days. Fat content does not change how fast milk sours, so there is no separate number to look up for your milk. The one exception is long-life milk, covered below, and that is a difference in processing, not in fat.

## Carton or bottle

CSIRO’s figure is for milk, not for a container. A two-litre carton and a glass bottle get the same five to seven days. What does change the number is where in the fridge it sits, which is the next section.

## What shortens the window

CSIRO’s number assumes the fridge is doing its job properly. A few things push milk toward the short end of five to seven days:

- **Door storage.** The door is the warmest part of the fridge — it swings in temperature every time it opens. Milk kept there will not last as long as milk kept on a middle shelf.
- **A fridge running warm.** Above 5°C, everything in the fridge ages faster than CSIRO’s number assumes. A cheap fridge thermometer settles the question in a day — [what temperature should your fridge be](/resources/what-temperature-should-your-fridge-be) has the number.
- **Time on the counter.** Every trip from the shop to the fridge, and every minute left out during breakfast, spends down a small piece of the five-to-seven-day window. It adds up faster than it looks.

## The signs worth trusting

A date on a carton is an estimate made before the milk existed. Your senses are reading the actual bottle in front of you, which is newer information:

- **Smell** is the most reliable test. Sour milk smells sour, unmistakably, before it looks any different.
- **A slight thickening or clumping** when poured is a clear sign, even if the smell has not fully turned yet.
- **Taste**, in a small amount, if the first two are unclear — a sip of milk that has just turned is unpleasant, not dangerous.

Milk that smells fine and looks fine is fine to drink, even a day past the date.

## Long-life vs fresh

UHT (long-life) milk plays by a different set of rules. Sterilised at a higher temperature, it can sit unrefrigerated and sealed for months. Opened, though, it becomes ordinary milk from that point on: back in the fridge, same five-to-seven-day clock. The long shelf life belongs to the unopened carton, not to the milk itself.

## Two bottles in the fridge

Milk gets bought weekly in most households, which is exactly how a new bottle and a six-day-old one end up side by side and indistinguishable. One from Tuesday’s shop, one bought Thursday “just in case” — the older one is the one that gets missed. A shared fridge makes it worse, not better: whoever bought the milk knows how old it is, and nobody else does.

This is the food waste that never makes a headline — not a dramatic bin clear-out, just five to seven days quietly running out with nobody watching the clock. Pantry fixes the part that goes wrong here. The moment a receipt is scanned, the date attaches to that bottle, not to someone’s memory of Tuesday, and every phone in the household sees the same age on the same bottle.
