Cooked chicken lasts three days in the fridge, per the [NSW Food Authority](https://www.foodauthority.nsw.gov.au/consumer/special-care-foods/leftovers), the same window it gives any other cooked leftover. That clock starts when the chicken is cooked and refrigerated, not when the raw pack came home, and it runs the same whether the chicken is a whole roast, a shredded breast or a hot chook from the supermarket. Three days, counted from the fridge door.

## Cooked on the last day of the raw window

Raw chicken has the shortest fridge life in a typical shop, [three days per CSIRO](/resources/chicken-in-the-fridge-three-days), and cooking it on day three is a legitimate way to use it. From the moment it is cooked and cooled, a new three-day window opens under the NSW Food Authority’s leftovers rule. Raw chicken bought Monday, cooked Wednesday night, is fine to eat through to Saturday. It is not a way to stretch raw chicken to six days — it is two separate clocks, and the second only starts when the first is honoured.

## A supermarket roast chicken

A hot roast chicken from Coles or Woolworths is a cooked leftover the moment it stops being dinner. Its three days count from when it goes into the fridge, and the time before that counts against it: the NSW Food Authority’s two-hour/four-hour rule puts food held between 5°C and 60°C for up to two hours back in the fridge safely, has it eaten straight away between two and four hours, and binned past four. A chook that rode home in a warm car, sat on the bench through dinner and went in at 10pm has already spent most of its safe bench time before the fridge clock starts. [The leftovers post](/resources/how-long-do-leftovers-keep) has that rule in full.

## Shredded, sliced or already in a dish

Pulling the meat off the bone does not change the number. Shredded chicken, sliced breast and the carcass all get the same three days, and smaller pieces go in the fridge sooner because they cool faster, which is the only advantage. Once the chicken is in a dish, the dish sets the clock: chicken in a curry is three days, chicken fried rice is two, because [cooked rice gets two days, not three](/resources/how-long-do-leftovers-keep), and the shorter number always wins.

## Do not trust the nose on this one

Raw chicken announces itself — a sour smell, a tacky surface. Cooked chicken often does not. Cooking changes how spoilage presents, and a container that still smells fine can be past the safe window, which is why the NSW Food Authority gives a number rather than a sniff test. Three days is the rule. A container that smells fine on day five is still a container on day five.

## Reheat it, or freeze it inside the window

Reheat cooked chicken until steaming hot, 60°C or above all the way through, per the NSW Food Authority — not warmed at the edges. If three days is not going to happen, the freezer is the answer before the window closes, not a fourth day in the fridge: frozen food stays safe indefinitely, per the Food Safety Information Council, and quality is the only limit — [freezer times, explained](/resources/freezer-times-explained). Chicken that was thawed and then cooked freezes again as a cooked dish without argument; the cautious answer to [can you refreeze it](/resources/defrosting-chicken-safely) has always been to cook it first.

## Why cooked chicken catches people out

A roast on Sunday is the most planned meal of the week, and the container of what was left is the least. It goes in without a label, behind the milk, and the plan for it is “sandwiches, probably”. Wednesday arrives before anyone circles back. That is the pattern behind most cooked leftovers that end up in the bin, and it is the container Pantry is built to catch: logged with a date and a countdown the moment it goes in, named in the 5:30pm nudge on its last day, and the starting point for [a dinner built from what is already in the house](/resources/what-to-cook-with-leftovers-before-they-go-off) rather than a recipe search.
